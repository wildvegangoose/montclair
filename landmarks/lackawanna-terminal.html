Lackawanna Railroad Terminal

A grand six-track Grecian-Doric terminal whose architect perished on the Titanic—and where Thomas Edison drove the first electric train in 1930.

📍 1 Lackawanna Plaza🏛 National Register of Historic Places (1973, #73001092)✏️ William Hull Botsford (Grecian-Doric)

Grecian-Doric main entrance of Lackawanna Terminal · Jim.henderson / Wikimedia Commons · CC0

William Hull Botsford's final masterpiece

When the Delaware, Lackawanna & Western Railroad eliminated grade crossings and modernized its Montclair Branch in 1912, it commissioned staff architect William Hull Botsford to design a monumental brick-and-concrete western terminal at Bloomfield Avenue and Spring Street (now Lackawanna Plaza). Botsford never lived to see it finished: he died aboard the RMS Titanic on April 15, 1912.

Opened in a grand town ceremony on June 28, 1913, the Grecian-Doric station boasted tapestry-bond brickwork, cast-marble trim, ornamental iron ticket windows, four platforms, six tracks and sweeping train sheds.

Edison's electric trains and life after rail

In September 1930 the Montclair Branch became the first electrified suburban commuter line in the region, with Thomas Edison himself at the controls of the inaugural electric train into Montclair. The terminal was added to the National Register of Historic Places on January 8, 1973.

Passenger service ended at the terminal on March 2, 1981, shifting east to Bay Street station so the Lackawanna and Erie lines could eventually be joined. Converted into Lackawanna Plaza in 1982 (long anchored by a supermarket until 2015), the historic station building has remained at the center of Montclair's downtown redevelopment debates ever since.

Did you know?

  • Architect William Hull Botsford died in the sinking of the RMS Titanic on April 15, 1912, a year before the terminal opened.
  • Opened June 28, 1913 with six tracks and four platforms; closed to trains March 2, 1981.
  • Thomas Edison drove the inaugural electric train on the Montclair Branch in September 1930.
  • Listed on the National Register of Historic Places on January 8, 1973 (#73001092).
